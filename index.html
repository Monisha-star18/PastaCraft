ABOUT PASTA

A book that embraces your love for pasta

Chapter One

History of Pasta

Noodle-like dishes go back thousands of years, but "pasta" as we know it took shape around the Mediterranean. Ancient Greek and Etruscan writings describe sheets of dough cut into strips — distant cousins of today's lasagna.

The popular tale of Marco Polo bringing noodles home from China in 1295 makes a fun story, but pasta was already being written about in Italy before he ever set sail.

01
Chapter Two

How Pasta Was Invented

The real breakthrough wasn't the dough — it was drying it. Arab traders and settlers in Sicily, around the 9th century, figured out how to dry wheat noodles in the sun so they'd survive long desert crossings without spoiling.

That single idea — turn fresh dough into a shelf-stable food — is what let pasta travel far beyond the kitchen it was made in, and it's still the difference between "fresh" and "dried" pasta on shelves today.

02
Chapter Three

Evolution of Pasta

By the 1600s–1700s, Naples had become a pasta capital, with sun-drenched streets used to dry long strands at scale. The invention of the mechanical press and extruder in the 1800s turned pasta-making from a slow hand craft into an industry.

Each new machine shape needed a new die, which is a big reason there are now well over 300 recognized pasta shapes — every one designed to hold a particular sauce a little differently.

03
World Tour · Italy

North vs. South

In Italy's north, cooler and richer in dairy and eggs, pasta traditionally means soft egg dough — tagliatelle, tortellini — rolled thin and often filled.

Head south and it's a different pantry: durum wheat and water, shaped into sturdy dried forms like orecchiette and spaghetti, built to hold up to olive oil and tomato.

04
World Tour · Asia

Noodles Came First

Long before pasta had a name, noodle-making was already old in Asia — archaeologists found a bowl of millet noodles in China dated to roughly 4,000 years ago.

The family tree branched wide from there: wheat udon and buckwheat soba in Japan, rice noodles across Vietnam and Thailand, each shaped by local grain and climate.

05
World Tour · Global

Pasta Goes Global

Waves of Italian immigrants carried pasta to the Americas in the 1800s and 1900s, where it picked up local habits — bigger portions, more meat, dishes like spaghetti and meatballs that barely exist in Italy itself.

Today pasta shows up everywhere from Japanese "napolitan" spaghetti to Nigerian jollof spaghetti — proof a simple dough of flour and water still has room to keep evolving.
